# Roadmap

Each milestone ends with something playable or testable. Spec pages in
`docs/spec/` must cover a system before it is implemented.

The primary reference is `docs/spec/rt3-clone-spec.md`; `docs/spec/gap-analysis.md`
lists everything it describes that is not built yet, and the open
decisions where our design differs from its proposals.

## M0: Foundations (done)
- Deterministic simulation core: calendar, integer money, PCG32 RNG,
  heightfield terrain with exact grades, fixed-step world tick.
- Cargo registry loaded from `data/cargo.json` (41 cargo types).
- Placeholder SDL2/OpenGL client; CI on gcc and clang.

## M1: Track and trains (in progress)
Done so far: track graph; a track planner with straight and curved runs,
grade-limited rail profiles, automatic bridges (wood/stone/steel) and
tunnels, and cost quotes; stations; locomotive data and loader; train
movement with grade and acceleration; looping routes; single-track meets by
priority; service towers and maintenance facilities with water, sand and oil;
breakdowns with a sandbox switch; age-based maintenance cost; a command
layer through which every player action passes; and build tools in the
client (track with preview, branching, stations, support buildings, trains);
the six game speeds on `+`, `−` and Pause (rt3-clone-spec §2); and the
spec's map scale: 0.5-mile cells, Small/Medium/Large maps, economy nodes of
several cells (about 15,000 a map) and catchment radii of 2/3/4 cells.
Provisional rules are listed in `docs/spec/m1-provisional-models.md`; their
numbers are in `data/balance.json`.

Done since: free undo (Ctrl+Z) of the last stations, towers, sheds and
station buildings placed, until leaving their tools, as for track;
track removal and double-track upgrades with refunds, taking down stations, towers and sheds, stations named after their town (and renamed with Enter in their panel), crashes,
yielding on a rival's track, and route editing: changing a train's stops,
manual service stops and priority (rt3-clone-spec §9.3), from Inspect
(E, H) and the Train tool.

Bridge, tunnel and overpass frequency settings (Never to Common, §11.2)
are done too: T, B and G in the Track tool.

Per-stop consists and custom cars can be set in the client too (Train
tool, rt3-clone-spec §9.3).

## M2: The economy (done)
Done in the first slice: the economy-node grid and cargo price field, with
off-rail drift of cargo along price gradients; industries and production
chains; houses; map population with towns and industries; a year of
history on new maps; and the RT3-style cargo price overlay in the client.
See `docs/spec/m2-economy-model.md`.

Done in the second slice: stations gather cargo from their catchment; trains
load automatically, unload at the first stop that pays more, and earn the
price difference less value lost in transit; delivered cargo feeds the local
economy.

Done in the third slice: passengers, mail and troops with destinations,
generated by houses and barracks for every connected station, with
distance fares, waiting loss and a mail demand cap.

Done in the fourth slice: terrain-aware middlemen (water and coasts conduct
prices and freight well, mountains poorly) and the five economic states,
which move production, costs, the prime rate and share prices.

Done in the fifth slice: industry ownership. Every producer and plant keeps
accounts; companies buy them at ten years' profit (or a floor), build
processing plants, and double their capacity; owned industries post their
profit to the owner; unowned ones that keep losing money close.

Done in the sixth slice: towns that grow with the service they get, with
star ratings; ports on the coast that receive, supply or exchange cargo;
and unowned ports and consumers that expand when demand fills them.

Done in the seventh slice: warehouses (inland ports that keep nearby cargo
from spoiling and earn what they save) and new industries appearing year by
year.

Done in the eighth slice: price-responsive industry output (a producer's
price falls as unsold stock piles up, and it slows), and post offices,
hotels, restaurants and taverns by stations.

Done in the ninth slice: calibration. Production follows the spec's rates
(about 2.2 loads a year for raw producers, 3 for processors), with
saturation, express and town growth rescaled to match, and a tool,
`railmaster_calibrate`, that scores the economy (now twelve metrics) against the spec's targets
([spec/calibration.md](spec/calibration.md)).

Done in the tenth slice: the spec's price field. Equilibrium prices come
from each node's demand and supply, prices relax over months, middlemen
carry stock toward every dearer neighbour, and the calibration tool checks
middleman speeds and how fast the price map reshapes.

Rival tycoons now value town pairs by the express code's own rates and
freight by the stations' catchment prices, so they build freight lines as
well as passenger ones (about a quarter of their revenue in the
calibration game). They also re-engine trains at 25 years, and each has
a recession caution that holds back building, borrowing and dividends in
bad times. Players can re-engine any of their trains (R in Inspect).

Consist rules (rt3-clone-spec §9.3): per-stop auto or custom consists with
minimum and maximum cars, waiting for full loads, caboose and dining car,
copying and retiring trains; empty cars are no longer hauled.

Done since: express units with homes (rt3-clone-spec §8.3 [D]).
Travellers set out from a house (or barracks) and go to one: a home's
travellers are shared by the stations that reach it rather than counted
again for each, they may get off at any station by their destination
home and change trains towards any of them, loads for different homes
bound the same way share cars, and a home whose travellers mostly gave up
avoids the railway for six months at every station, new ones included.
The travel overlay draws where they are going by town. See
[spec/m2-economy-model.md](spec/m2-economy-model.md) and, for the
calibration, [spec/calibration.md](spec/calibration.md).

Nothing left in M2 from the spec's list; the research backlog below
would sharpen its numbers.

Later eras are calibrated too: the demo network started in 1900 and 1950
runs the era's fastest steam or diesel engine and returns about what it
does from 1830, helped by the spec's era factor on passenger and mail
spawning and rarer breakdowns ([spec/calibration.md](spec/calibration.md)).

Done since: industry footprints of 4 × 4 cells (rt3-clone-spec §3.1): a
station reaching any part of an industry's yard reaches it, and
footprints never overlap; the client draws each yard fenced and cleared of
trees, with trodden earth round the buildings.

Done before that: territories (rt3-clone-spec §3.3): access rights to build,
territory fees, station-cost, overhead and credit modifiers, closed borders
for middlemen, a generator for new maps and rivals that buy access.

## M3: Company and finance (in progress)
Done in the first slice: company cash that pays for everything (refusing
what it cannot afford, except in sandbox); revenue by line; monthly running
costs (train maintenance, fuel, track and building upkeep, interest); yearly
income statements; balance sheet and book value; credit rating; bonds; a
finance screen in the client. See `docs/spec/m3-finance-model.md`.

Done in the second slice: personal account and net worth, salary, shares
traded in 1,000-share blocks with price impact, margin with interest and
forced sales, stock issues (twice a year) and buybacks, quarterly dividends,
and a monthly share-price model.

Done in the third slice: rival companies. Every track piece, station,
building and train has an owner; trains may run on rivals' track and use
their stations, paying trackage; the market covers every company; and AI
tycoons with personalities build and run their own lines through the same
commands as the player. See `docs/spec/rivals-model.md`.

Done in the fourth slice: short selling, takeovers (a shareholder vote
for the chair) and mergers (buying out a rival's shareholders and absorbing
everything it owns), used by the player from the market screen and by the
AI according to personality.

Done in the fifth slice: investor sentiment from yearly shareholder
returns, grumbling and votes to fire a chairman, resigning, successors,
salary set by the five-year return, and stock splits.

Done in the sixth slice: the founding dialog (your investment and outside
money, shares at $10), founding a new company after losing one, 1%
brokerage, and voluntary bankruptcy.

Done in the seventh slice: the spec's share-price model (book value,
earnings trend at a P/E set by the economy, dividends weighted by an
unbroken record, revenue; 1/8 smoothing; trade pressure that grows with the
square root of a trade and fades) and its ten-grade credit rating, scored
from asset cover, interest cover, profit record, bonds and bankruptcy, with
bonds at prime plus the grade's spread.

Done in the eighth slice: the spec's ageing (maintenance +4% of the new
cost a year, half again with no oil; breakdowns rising with age like
crashes) and track upkeep at 2% of cost a year.

Not planned: depreciation. The spec's balance sheet carries track,
buildings and trains at their value and names no depreciation, so book
value (the share price's anchor) does not fall with age.

## M4: Real 3D client
Started: the client draws the map in 3D (fixed-function OpenGL): a lit
terrain mesh with smooth ground colours, rocky slopes, flat water, trees,
houses with roofs, industries with chimneys, track at rail height with
bridges on piers, stations, and trains as boxes following the track. The
camera orbits a point on the ground (turn, tilt, zoom; rt3-clone-spec
§15.6) and the mouse picks by casting its ray onto the terrain, so every
tool works as before. V switches to the old top-down map.

Close up, track is ballast with sleepers and two rails (following the
ground through cuttings), bridges are decks on piers coloured by type, and
tunnels show their mouths. Trains are built from shapes: steam engines
with boiler, chimney, cab and tender; diesel and electric hoods (with a
pantograph); coaches, mail vans, dining cars, boxcars, hoppers for coal,
iron and bauxite, and a caboose.

Stations have platforms, a canopy and a station house in the owner's
colours (longer when bigger); water towers, engine sheds, hotels,
restaurants, taverns and post offices have their own shapes. Industries
are shaped by type: mines with headframes and spoil heaps, farms with
barns, silos and fields (orchards with trees), oil derricks, tanks at
chemical works and refineries, furnaces and stacks at steel and aluminium
mills, power stations and a nuclear cooling tower, ports with a quay and
crane, warehouses, offices and factories with sawtooth roofs. Views can
be bookmarked (Shift+0-9, 0-9), and a radar shows the whole map.

Town houses keep clear of track, stations and water (each house tries a
few spots and is left out if the railway has them all). Moving steam
engines puff smoke, works and power-station chimneys smoke, and the
nuclear cooling tower steams. Moving the mouse to the window edge scrolls
the view.

The ground is textured (a detail texture made in code, no asset files:
blotches and speckle like grass and earth, multiplied with the land's lit
colours; ripples on water), with box-filtered mipmaps so far ground does
not shimmer. Distant land fades into a sky-coloured haze. Smoke stops
while the game is paused.

Towns are laid out on a grid of streets centred on each town (rows east-west
or north-south by town): its houses fill the plots nearest the centre, with
a ragged edge and the odd garden, clear of the railway, industries and
water. Buildings face their street with windows on each floor and a door:
cottages, two-storey houses and terraced rows outside, shops and offices
with awnings in the middle (taller as the town grows) and a church with a
spire. Streets are dirt, then gravel from 1890 and asphalt from 1930. Streets may cross plain track, but stop short of
stations and railway buildings, leaving an open square round each.

Rolling stock is modelled: steam engines with red spoked drivers, a pony
truck, smokebox, flared chimney, brass steam dome, sand dome, headlamp,
buffer beam, a windowed cab and a tender heaped with coal; road-switcher
diesels (hood, cab, nose, stripe, exhaust) and box-cab electrics with a
pantograph, on bogies; coaches with rows of lit windows and a clerestory,
mail vans with doors, ribbed boxcars with a door, open hoppers showing
their load (coal, iron or bauxite), and a caboose with its cupola.

Stations are modelled by size: a small one is a wooden depot with a lean-to
canopy over its one platform; a medium one has platforms both sides, a
two-storey brick house and a canopy on posts; a large one has long
canopied platforms, a stone hall with wings and a clock tower, and a
footbridge. Platforms have painted edges and lamps, and a name board in
the owner's colours stands at the end.

The countryside is wooded: groves and woods spread by noise over the grass
(the generator places no forest of its own yet), single trees between,
broadleaves with rounded crowns and stands of two-tier conifers, all clear
of the railway, industries, towns and water. Trees are built in 32x32-tile
chunks as they come into view and drawn only near the camera; what stands
where and the town layouts are cached and rebuilt only when the railway or
the towns change. The view opens on the biggest town. The client draws at
full resolution on Retina screens, with 4x anti-aliasing, and follows the
window's real size (a Mac may open it smaller than asked).

Shading: GLSL 1.20 shaders on the OpenGL 2.1 context (functions loaded at
run time) light every pixel with sunlight, a sky-and-ground ambient and the
haze, and a 2048x2048 shadow map from the sun gives buildings, trees,
stations, trains and hills soft-edged shadows over the area round the
camera (wider as it pulls back, fading out at its edge). A gradient sky
replaces the flat colour. `--no-shaders`, or a system without shaders or
framebuffers, keeps the fixed-function lighting.

Art pipeline: glTF models (`.glb`/`.gltf`, read with cgltf, textures with
stb_image) listed by role in `data/models/models.json` replace the shapes
drawn in code for town houses, terraces, shops, churches, station
buildings, engines, cars and each industry; empty roles keep the code's
shapes (docs/models.md). Models are fitted to their plot, track or site,
turned to face the street or track, and lit and shadowed by the shaders
(which recover map positions through the inverse view, so models keep
their own transforms). Engines and cars come from Kenney's Train Kit
(CC0), freight cars by cargo (coal, ore, logs, lumber, tank wagons), and
the woods from Kenney's Nature Kit (CC0), recoloured by material name to
the game's greens. Town buildings are assembled from Kenney's Fantasy
Town Kit (CC0) walls and roofs as prefabs (tools/models/town_prefabs.py):
timber and stone cottages, houses, terraces, shops and a church with a
spire, on period recolourings of the kit's palette. Stations are
assembled the same way (a timber depot, a two-storey station, a hall with
a clock tower), as are a windmill beside grain and corn farms and a
watermill for the lumber mill. The player's engines are always the green
locomotive, rivals' red or blue, and each flies a pennant in its owner's
colour. Mills, factories, works, plants, warehouses and barracks come
from Kenney's City Kit (Industrial) (CC0) on a brick recolouring of its
palette, as do water towers and engine sheds; mines, farms, logging camps,
oil wells and ports keep their code-drawn headframes, fields and derricks,
with props beside them from Kenney's Survival Kit (log piles, ore heaps,
barrels and crates). Water towers and engine sheds stand clear of other
track, each building at a node in its own spot.

Code-drawn industries in more detail: mines have a braced timber
headframe with its winding wheel and rope, a brick engine house with a
smoking stack, and spoil heaps; oil wells a tapering lattice derrick with
girts and braces, an engine shed and storage tanks; farms ploughed fields
in furrows (or pasture and orchards in planted rows) inside split-rail
fences, a red gambrel barn, a domed silo and a farmhouse, and herds of
cattle (black and white at dairies) or sheep. New primitives: beams
between any two points, gambrel roofs, fences.

Electrified track has overhead line close up: steel masts beside the
track every few hundred feet, arms with droppers out over each track,
and the contact wire sagging a little between them.

Bridges are drawn by type: timber trestles (splayed bents with girts and
X-braces, stringers under the deck), stone viaducts (piers with battered
footings, round arches under spandrel walls, a parapet), steel through
trusses (chords, posts and Warren diagonals either side, braced overhead,
on end piers) and suspension bridges (towers at the ends of the whole
crossing, main cables sagging between them, hangers to a stiffening
girder).

Ports find the water and build out to it: a stone quay, a warehouse, a
timber wharf on piles, a crane with its jib, rope and load, and a
two-masted schooner moored alongside. Logging camps have a log cabin with
a smoking chimney, an open saw shed over its pit, a log deck stacked in a
pyramid and stumps all round. Tunnel mouths have a stone headwall with a
round-arched mouth, a ring of voussoirs, a coping and splayed wing walls.

Interface: text is set in Source Sans 3 (OFL), baked with stb_truetype
into a glyph atlas per size at the screen's pixel density (the old 5x7
pixel font remains as a fallback), in sentence case rather than capitals;
town ratings show as gold stars. Panels, bars, toolbar tabs, labels and
the radar share one look (src/client/ui.cpp): warm dark gradients framed in
brass with soft shadows, raised brass tabs for the chosen tool; town names
are placed nearest first, left out where they would overlap, and fade with
distance. Stations carry signs too: the name, a stripe in the owner's colour,
and pips counting the loads of passengers, mail and freight waiting, for
the stations near the camera. On the platforms themselves, what waits is drawn too:
a figure for every quarter load of passengers (up to two dozen) in coats
of many colours, mail sacks at one end and freight crates stacked at the
other. Resting the mouse on a train, station or industry in the Look
tool brings up a card of its main facts beside the cursor, and the view
glides (rather than jumps) to what the lists, Find, the Guide and the
radar choose. The Train tool has a panel: the engines to click, the
stops so far, buttons for each stop's cars, cargo, full loads and
service, and Buy; the Track and Station tools have panels of their
options (double track, curves, tunnel, bridge and overpass frequency,
undo; station size and what to build, with prices; the Industry tool's
plants to build), and the Company
and Market screens a row of buttons for their actions. Stations are picked by where they are drawn on screen,
so far ones near the horizon are as easy to click as near ones. Zoom by
wheel, smooth trackpad scrolling,
pinch, or Option (Alt) + Up/Down.

Clouds' shadows (shaders; off in Settings) drift over the land, dimming
only the sunlight; cover comes and goes over a few minutes, and at its
thickest it rains (streaks, a duller light and the sound of rain) or, in
winter, snows.

Water (shaders): ripples drifting with the wind, the sky mirrored at low
angles and the sun glinting, calmer far off; ice in midwinter. A body of
water lies at one level (lowered across neighbours within a small step),
with earth banks up to the shore and between rapids, so no gaps show.

Seasons (shaders only; off in Settings): autumn turns the broadleaf trees
gold to red and fades the grass from mid September, and snow lies on
open ground, the trees and sloped roofs from late November into March,
patchy at the edges. Only green surfaces change, so track, roads, roofs and water stay
readable; conifers keep their needles.

Time of day (N): day, dusk, night and dawn, or a slow round of all four
(six minutes while the game runs). The sun (or moon), its colour, the sky
and ground ambient, the sky gradient and the haze change with it, in the
shaders and the fixed-function path alike; shadows follow the light.
After dark the sky has twinkling stars and a moon with a soft glow,
turning with the view.
After dark, windows light up along the town's streets, station lamps
glow, and engines carry headlamps with a pool of light on the track
ahead; smoke and track darken with the rest.

Ambient occlusion (shaders): what covers the land (trees, houses,
industries, stations) is drawn into a coverage map a few texels a tile and
blurred; the ground's sky light and some of its sunlight dim by it, and
leaves by half as much, so woods have dark floors and buildings stand in a
soft pool rather than floating. Walls, trunks and wheels darken by it too
where they meet the ground, fading out over their first few feet (the
shader reads the land's height from a 16-bit texture of the map's corners). It is rebuilt with the scene on a worker
thread, so building track never stalls.

Speed: woods keep two display lists a chunk, model trees near the camera
and the light code-drawn ones further off; the detailed track (beds,
sleepers, rails) and the towns, streets and industries are compiled into
display lists rebuilt only when the railway or the scene changes (the
chimneys' smoke is recorded with them and replayed each frame). F12 shows
frames a second.

Simulation speed: the price field's daily relaxation remembers the power
it takes of each demand-to-supply ratio (exactly, so saves and replays are
unchanged), which made a large map with three rivals run about 1.8 times
faster (about 80 game days a second in its first year, 58 by its fifth,
against the 6 Very Fast needs).

Town houses come in several shapes, all from the kit's pieces: plain
gables, L-shaped houses with a one-storey back wing, cottages with a cross
gable, hipped roofs, and square two-by-two Foursquares under a pyramid
roof. Cottages and two-storey houses draw from separate lists (the
`cottage` and `house` roles).

Behind each house is a garden out to halfway to the next row: picket
fences, hedges or stone walls round it (brick behind terraces), and a
shed, a vegetable bed or an apple tree as the plot has them; shops have a
walled yard with a store. Houses in towns whose streets run north-south
now face their street (they had faced away from it).

Churches stand in a walled churchyard with rows of headstones and a yew.
Towns of 14 houses or more keep the block by their centre as a green:
mown grass, gravel paths crossing to a bandstand or a monument, and trees
down both sides, with streets all round (only where the railway, an
industry or water leaves it clear).

Towns of 30 houses or more pave that block as a square instead: flagstones,
a fountain in a stone basin, market stalls under striped awnings, a lamp
(lit after dark) and a tree at each corner.

Towns of 10 houses or more have iron street lamps along their streets,
by turns either side, lit after dark.

Towns of 20 houses or more (two from 30) have grounds on their edges,
up a lane beyond their last houses where the land is clear: allotments
(fenced plots of crops and earth, some with sheds), a walled cemetery
(rows of headstones and crosses, a chapel, yews) or a ball ground (mown
in stripes, a boundary line and pitch, a pavilion, a picket fence). What
stands on a green, square or ground stands on its surface (it had been
sunk into it), and street lamps stand on the ground beside the street.

Town streets stop at water rather than running across it: a stream or
river up to 1.6 tiles wide with land beyond is crossed by a stone bridge
(parapets, piers into the water); wider water, where the bank is low,
gets a landing at the street's end (a jetty on piles, a boathouse and a
boat), up to three to a town.

Towns of 25 houses or more have a town hall, the shop nearest their
centre: stone, two cells deep under twin gables, a clock tower over the
door with a tall spire (the `town_hall` role; drawn in code without it).
Shops also come as deeper blocks under twin gables (two or three storeys).

Town growth is seen: a house that appears while the game runs goes up
over 90 days (a stone foundation, timber walls rising, studs and a plate
above the planking, scaffolding round them, rafters at the end, timber
stacked by the plot), with no one in it after dark until it is done. The
client tracks this itself (houses standing when a game is started or
loaded were never building sites).

Towns of 10 houses or more have pale raised pavements either side of
their streets (the lamps stand on them), stopping short of the crossings;
towns of 30 or more cobble their streets until asphalt comes in 1930.

Town streets carry traffic, drawn each frame near the camera: a vehicle
or two on each street going back and forth on its side of the road, more
in larger towns. Covered wagons behind a horse at first; motor cars mix in
from 1905 and are all there is by 1925, with lorries from 1915; their
headlamps light the streets after dark. They stand still while the game
is paused.

People are about the towns too (as passengers already wait on platforms,
a figure for every quarter load): walking the pavements, two to four to a
street by the town's size; shopping among a square's stalls and walking
round its fountain; strolling a green's paths. Most go home after dark.

Boats work the wider water near the camera, each going back and forth
along a straight stretch of open water: sailing boats and barges, steam
launches and tugs (smoking) from 1860, motor boats from 1910; ducks paddle
by the town landings.

Finding towns: the Lists (F11) have a Towns page, biggest first, with
their stars, houses, your stations there and miles from the view (Enter
goes there). Town names carry further the bigger the town, and where
labels crowd it is the small towns' that give way.

Shift+M opens the big map: the radar across the window with every town
named, industries marked by kind, and your lines; a click goes there.

On it, every railroad's track shows in its colour (yours white, as in the
world) with a key, and Tab adds the Guide's ten best unserved lines,
dashed in gold and numbered, with what each would earn.

Clicking one of those lines (or its number), or pressing its number key,
goes to it with the line planned as the Guide's Enter does: B prices it,
B again builds it.

The Guide puts lines a rival already runs (its stations reach both ends)
after the open ones, dimmed and marked with who runs them; the big map's
best lines leave them out.

A train's panel shows its legs: for each run into a stop this year (or
last, early on), the trips, what a trip earned, and the profit after its
share of the train's running costs by distance; losing legs show red with
a hint to change what is loaded there. The simulation keeps them (saves
from version 25).

The Ledger's train list shows a train with a losing leg in red, naming
the worst leg.

Each leg says what it earned from (its two best cargoes) and how many of
its trips sold nothing there (saves from version 26). On the demo most
trips sell nothing, and a closer look showed why: its two trains call
every six days or so, faster than the towns fill a car (express loads go
when half a car waits), so they mostly leave empty.

Measured (three maps, 18 months, the demo's two trains): waiting for a
full load does not pay. As built earned most on two maps of three;
waiting for one car at every stop earned 1 to 14% less, two cars 34 to
40% less, and one train instead of two 13% less to 7% more. Running
empty is cheap and fares fall with the time a trip takes, so the hint
pointing to W was taken out; the panel just counts the trips that sold
nothing.

The help card (?) has a second page (Tab): what makes a line pay, from
these measurements.

A long-game check: a scripted player following the tips (towns 5 to 16
miles apart, then up to 30; a train a line, a second on good ones; a
dividend once it makes $250K) on three maps with three rivals, to 1850.
The old ladder had six of its seven goals met within the first year
(shares trade at twice the founding price from the start, so "double the
share price" came free) and then nothing until $2M a year, which this
player never reached: linking towns alone levels off at $0.5M to $1.5M.
The ladder is now ten goals: the first year's basics, a dividend, eight
towns and $1M a year (over a full year) from about 1831, 100 loads of
freight about 1837 to 1841, and $2M a year, which needs freight and
industries.

Is $2M a year in reach? Run to 1860 beside the same player, the rival AIs
(which also build freight lines, add trains freely and buy industries)
got there: on one map two of three rivals passed $2M by 1838 and reached
$5-6M by 1860; on another one passed it by 1850 and another reached
$3.8M by 1860. The town-linking player stayed at $0.9-1.6M. So the last
goal sits where its hint says, on freight and industries, about 1838 to
1855 for a player who builds them.

The Guide now prices freight links as the AI does, at the best price a
station at each end would reach (sim::best_price_within_reach, shared).
Tested on 18 links over three maps against a first year's actual freight
income: the sites' own prices overstated it by about 13%, the reach
prices were within 2%. The ranking hardly changed (the two methods' top
picks earned the same within 1%); the figures shown are now honest.

Why livestock led the Guide on every map: every raw producer makes about
2.2 loads a year (spec 6.1) and livestock's median price is $90k against
$30-45k for most raw cargo (spec 4.1), so its best link was worth about
$310k a year against $105-190k for the others (four maps). That is the
spec's economy and stays. The Guide now shows variety instead: at most
three freight links of a cargo rank among the open ones before the rest
of that cargo; town pairs are not capped.

Chains in the Guide: freight into a works shows what the works would
make of it ("Livestock > Meat"), and for the chosen line, the best place
to carry that on and what it could bring. Measured (both legs built, on
three maps): nothing the first year; by the fourth, two of three meat
legs earned within 5% of the estimate ($137K for $133K, $120K for
$125K), the third nothing that year; the works made 1.4 to 1.6 loads a
year, under the 2.2 fed. So the Guide says "up to", "once it runs (a
year or two)", and the chain does not lift a line's rank.

The chosen chain line also says how the works stands now: idle and
waiting for this line, or running at some percent and holding so many
loads, its product ready to carry.

Shade between buildings (shaders): each town building is told, side by
side, the nearest neighbour across from each wall (how tall, how far off,
how much of the wall it faces), and its walls lose sky light by the angle
that neighbour hides, most at their foot, so alleys and the gaps between
houses darken. A soft band of shade runs under every building's eaves (for
the art, where its roof's sloping faces start).

Houses far off (discrete LOD): the town buildings that come from the art
are kept in display lists by 16-tile chunks, two each: the art near the
camera, the same buildings drawn in code further off (a few dozen
triangles each against a few thousand). On a wide tilted view of the
quick game, trees hidden, under software rendering a frame took 231 ms
against 456-461 ms with the art everywhere; far off the two look alike.
(People and boats in towns were drawn twice a frame; now once.)
Tried and left out: the same for the industries' art. Measured the same
way it saved nothing (250-257 ms against 231 ms with only houses
switched, within the runs' noise), and the code-drawn industries look
different far off (smaller, some buildings missing), so industries keep
their art at every distance. Checked too: held still, a town at the
centre of a mid-distance view is the same with the art everywhere and with
houses switched by distance (the switch falls well beyond it).

Contact shadows: street lamps, people and carts and cars each stand on a
soft dark spot fading out from their foot, following the slope, so they
sit on the ground even where the sun's shadow map is too coarse to show
them. The spots are drawn before what stands on them and not written to
depth: at a town's distance the depth buffer cannot tell a spot from the
feet on it, and written, the spots hid the people (seen, then fixed).

Trains stand on a soft strip of shade too, the length of the engine and
its cars, fading at the sides and ends, on the rails or the ground beside
(on a bridge, its deck), drawn the same way before the trains.

Getting around: in the Look tool the map drags with the left button (a
press that does not move is still a click, on release), a double click
goes to the place (on or near a town, in close), the + and - by the
minimap zoom (held, they keep going), Cmd (Ctrl) + = and - zoom, and the
settings say which way a scroll zooms. Asked for by a player on a Mac
trackpad, where pinching may not reach the game.

A compass under the zoom buttons points north as the view turns; a click
on it, or Home, puts the view back to north-up at the usual tilt.

Find has a button on the top bar too, beside the Guide's: going to a town
or station by name needs no keys.

The names on the map are clickable in the Look tool: a town's goes there,
closer in; a station's opens its panel.

The goals banner says it folds ("hide tip" at its right): a click tucks
it to a single line, and back (it always did, but nothing said so). The
first steps' panel already had its own "hide".

Trackpads work like a map app: two fingers slide the map (a scroll in
fine steps or sideways is a trackpad's; whole notches are a wheel's,
which zooms), a pinch zooms at the pointer (on macOS read from the
system's magnify events, as SDL2 does not pass them on;
`src/client/gestures_mac.mm`), Cmd + scroll zooms, a flick carries on a
little, and Settings can make every scroll zoom or move. Asked for by a
player on a Mac trackpad.

From the same play-testing: a train's panel now has buttons for what can
be done with it (change route, new engine, priority, fewer or more cars,
copy, wait for full loads, follow, retire), and your station's for
enlarging and renaming, so nothing needs a key to be found; a Menu
button on the top bar opens the game menu (settings such as music,
saving, the main menu, quitting), which only Esc reached before; and
Normal speed is half as fast, about eight minutes a game year. In a window too
narrow for every top-bar button with its key (about 1100 pixels), the
buttons drop the keys ("Look", "Guide", "Menu") so they all still fit.

Multiplayer's network (`src/net`, lockstep): `railmaster --host` opens a
new game with four seats, and `railmaster --join=ADDRESS` joins it. The
guest is sent the host's game as a save and takes the first free seat.
From then on the host sends, every frame, how far its game has run and
the commands given since (its own and the guests'), and each guest's copy
plays on that far at the host's pace. A guest's own commands go to the
host, which plays them for everyone; the guest's copy waits for the
command to come back (a few milliseconds on a home network) and answers
with the result it got playing it itself (`World::set_remote`). So the
game's tools work unchanged, including the ones that chain commands on
what the last one made (track, then a station on its end, then a train
for the station). Once a game month the host sends a checksum of its game
(`World::checksum`), and a guest that no longer matches says so. Tests
run a host and a guest over the loopback: founding, bonds, a refused
command, a whole railway built by the guest, the copies matching byte for
byte after 90 days (three checksums), a copy changed behind the host's
back being found out, a full game turning a guest away, and the host
leaving (the guest's game carries on alone). Checked by hand with two
game windows: a guest founding its company through the host, both papers
reporting it, five months at Very Fast with five checksums matched. A
bug found on the way: the host sent a tick's go-ahead before its
checksum, so guests could pass the tick unchecked; the test now counts
the checksums matched.

Then Play together on the title screen: your name, Host a new game (the
new railroad's settings, with seats for four) and Join at an address
typed in, remembered between games; the host's top line shows where to
join and how many play, a guest's the host's speed or that the host has
paused. Players go by their names (a RenamePlayer command, logged and
played on every copy like any other), so the papers say "Ann has founded
...". Checked with two game windows, from the title screens to both
papers.

Then chat: Ctrl+T or the Chat button (shown only in multiplayer) opens a
line to type in; the host passes each line on with who said it, and the
last lines show above the bottom bar for half a minute, kept in the
message history. The Status screen lists the players: name, railroad, net
worth, and whether each is in the game now. A test sends a line each way;
checked with two game windows.

A hosted game goes on another day: Play together's Host a saved game
loads it and opens it again, and a player joining under the name they
played by takes their seat (and railroad) back; newcomers take a seat
nobody has made their own. Tested over the loopback and with two game
windows (one evening hosted, saved, the next hosted from the title
screen, the guest back in their railroad).

Building for the Mac in CI (an Apple-silicon job: build, tests, and
share.sh's app checked to start with Homebrew's SDL hidden, kept as the
run's download) turned up what Linux's newer compilers let pass: a local
named like a field in a static member (Apple's clang 15 calls it
shadowing), and structured bindings captured by lambdas (clang 16 and
later). Building with clang 15 and libc++ here found them all at once.
It also turned up a real bug: gcc and clang made different worlds from
the same seed. Three places drew two random numbers in one expression
(town and territory names, territory seeds), whose order C++ leaves to
the compiler. Each draw is now a statement of its own; loaded games
always ran alike (400 days compared), so multiplayer, which starts every
guest from the host's save, was not affected, but maps from a seed and
replays were. A CI job now has gcc's and clang's builds make the same
game byte for byte and replay each other's (railmaster_replay --make);
put back, the old code fails it. Homebrew's sdl2 has become sdl2-compat
(over SDL3, loaded as it starts), so share.sh puts SDL3 in the app too.

Farm fields round the towns, as RT3's countryside has: a ragged ring of
open, gentle grassland about each town (wider for bigger towns) is laid
out in fields, a tile split into one, two or four, each with its crop
(wheat, barley, roots, pasture, hay) and furrows running one way or the
other, hedges round the farmland and along half the edges inside it, and
no trees on it. The crops follow the year (green, ripe, stubble,
ploughed, under snow). Decoration only: the simulation knows nothing of
them. Drawn as one quad a field with a furrow texture (a first version
in strips came to about a million quads on a large map), kept out of the
shadow pass. A first version let the grass show through in leaf shapes
along each tile's diagonal: lifted too little above the ground, which
the terrain draws as two flat triangles a tile.

Country roads between the towns: each town to its two nearest within 45
tiles, found by least cost over the land (round water and industries,
easy on the grades), smoothed into curves and joined to the nearest
street at either end; dirt, gravel, then asphalt with the era, as the
streets; fields and trees keep off them. Decoration only. The routes are
kept until the land, the towns' places or the industries change (a large
map's 195 roads take about a tenth of a second to find), so laying track
or a town growing (both rebuild the scene) costs only the trimming,
about 6 ms. Carts and, as the years go by, motor cars and lorries travel
them (about one to five tiles of road, near the camera), drawn by the
same code as the towns' traffic. Still to do: road bridges (a road with
no way round water is left out).
